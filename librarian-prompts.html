AI Prompts for Librarians Supporting OER and ZTC

Use AI to reduce the repetitive research and communication work around faculty requests, while keeping acquisitions, licensing, accessibility, and local access decisions in librarian hands.

The Librarian Still Makes the Library Decision

These prompts are designed to help with research, comparison, organization, and communication. AI should not invent institutional prices, user models, vendor availability, license terms, or accessibility claims.

Use your library systems, vendors, licenses, catalog, and professional judgment to verify anything that affects actual student access.

What Are You Trying to Do?

📥 Triage a Faculty Request Turn a vague request into the few questions and searches that matter. 🔎 Investigate a Specific Title Organize public evidence before checking your local vendors. ↔ Find Better Alternatives When the requested title has a poor access model, search for substitutes. ⚖️ Compare Access Options Organize price, user model, sustainability, and restrictions. ✉ Reply to Faculty Turn technical findings into a clear, collegial recommendation.
Librarian 1

Triage a Faculty ZTC Request

Use this when a faculty request is broad, such as “Can the library get something for my class?” or “Can you make this book ZTC?”

🌱 Quick Prompt

Best when you need to turn a messy email into a short research plan.

I am an academic librarian supporting a faculty request related to OER or Zero Textbook Cost (ZTC).

FACULTY REQUEST
[PASTE THE REQUEST]

Help me triage it.

Identify:
1. What the faculty member appears to be asking for
2. What information I already have
3. The 1–3 missing details that would materially affect my search
4. What I should check in my local library systems or vendor tools
5. What could be researched publicly before I do that

Do not invent institutional access, prices, licenses, or vendor availability.

If the faculty member is not committed to one exact title, suggest a broader search goal based on the instructional need.

End with a short checklist I can use to work the request.
🚀 Deeper Workflow

Best when the request may involve several formats, vendors, or possible substitutes.

Act as a research assistant helping an academic librarian triage a faculty OER/ZTC request.

FACULTY REQUEST
[PASTE REQUEST]

KNOWN COURSE CONTEXT
[COURSE, ENROLLMENT, TERM, CURRENT TEXT, TOPIC, OR OTHER INFORMATION]

PHASE 1 — DEFINE THE INSTRUCTIONAL NEED
Separate:
- exact-title preference
- actual instructional purpose
- required format or use
- approximate scale of student access needed
- timing/deadline

If the request is vague, identify no more than 3 questions worth asking the faculty member.

PHASE 2 — DEFINE THE LIBRARY CHECKS
Create a local verification checklist for:
- current holdings
- vendor availability
- user/access model
- price
- perpetual vs. subscription terms
- DRM/download restrictions
- accessibility information
- platform requirements
- sustainability across future terms

PHASE 3 — PUBLIC RESEARCH
Identify useful public research that can be done before or alongside local vendor work, such as:
- publisher information
- academic-library platform evidence
- examples of similar ZTC use at other colleges
- alternative titles serving the same instructional need

PHASE 4 — PRIORITIZE
Tell me the most efficient order in which to do the work.

Do not invent any local-library fact or vendor term.

OUTPUT
Give me:
A. Faculty need
B. Questions worth asking
C. Local library checks
D. Public research
E. Best next action
Librarian 2

Investigate a Specific Requested Title

Use this to organize public evidence before you check your own catalog, vendor platforms, or acquisitions systems.

🌱 Quick Prompt

Best when you want a clean public-evidence brief.

I am an academic librarian investigating this requested title:

Title: [TITLE]
Author: [AUTHOR]
Publisher: [OPTIONAL]
ISBN: [OPTIONAL]

Find reliable public evidence related to:
- publisher ebook availability
- academic-library ebook platform listings or references
- college or university library catalog records
- documented course or ZTC use at another institution, if available

For each source, tell me exactly what the evidence supports.

Do not infer:
- my library's access
- institutional price
- unlimited-user availability
- DRM-free status
- perpetual rights
- any other license term that is not explicitly public

Create a short table:
Source | What It Shows | Link | What It Does NOT Prove

End with a checklist of what I still need to verify locally.
🚀 Deeper Workflow

Best when you want to compare public evidence with the instructional role of the title.

I am an academic librarian preparing to investigate a faculty-requested ebook.

TITLE
[TITLE / AUTHOR / ISBN / PUBLISHER]

COURSE USE
[COURSE, APPROXIMATE ENROLLMENT, HOW THE TITLE WILL BE USED]

PUBLIC RESEARCH TASK
Find reliable public evidence for:
1. Publisher ebook availability
2. Academic-library platform presence or references
3. Other academic-library catalog records
4. Documented ZTC/course use at other institutions
5. Publicly stated access-model information, only if it is genuinely documented

EVIDENCE STANDARD
For every finding, separate:
- Verified public fact
- Reasonable lead for local checking
- Unverified assumption

Do not use consumer prices as institutional prices.

Do not claim a title has an unlimited-user, perpetual, DRM-free, or other license unless the evidence explicitly supports that claim.

OUTPUT
Create:

PUBLIC EVIDENCE TABLE
Source | Evidence | Confidence | Local Follow-Up

INSTRUCTIONAL ROLE
Briefly summarize what the title appears to be doing for the course.

LOCAL VERIFICATION CHECKLIST
Include:
- existing holdings
- vendor/platform availability
- access model
- institutional price
- simultaneous users
- perpetual/subscription status
- DRM/download limits
- accessibility
- platform requirements
- alternatives with better access

End with the 3 most important things to check first.
Librarian 3

Find Alternatives When the Requested Title Will Not Work

Use this when the exact title is unavailable, too restrictive, too costly, or otherwise impractical for whole-class use.

🌱 Quick Prompt

Best when you need a few substitute titles that meet the same instructional need.

I am an academic librarian helping a faculty member find an alternative to this requested title:

[REQUESTED TITLE]

The book is being used for:
[TOPIC / UNIT / INSTRUCTIONAL PURPOSE]

The reason I need alternatives is:
[POOR ACCESS MODEL / UNAVAILABLE / COST / OTHER]

Find 5–8 commercially published or openly licensed alternatives that serve the same instructional purpose.

For each option, provide:
- Title and author
- Publisher and year
- Why it may work as a substitute
- Evidence of academic-library or OER availability, if verifiable
- What I still need to check locally

Do not assume that a similar title has a better license.

Do not invent institutional prices or access models.

Prioritize pedagogical fit over superficial title similarity.

End with the 3 alternatives most worth checking first.
🚀 Deeper Workflow

Best when you want alternatives ranked by instructional fit and likelihood of practical library access.

I am an academic librarian searching for alternatives to a requested title that is not practical for whole-class ZTC use.

REQUESTED TITLE
[TITLE / AUTHOR]

INSTRUCTIONAL FUNCTION
[WHAT THE FACULTY MEMBER NEEDS STUDENTS TO READ, LEARN, ANALYZE, OR DO]

KNOWN PROBLEM
[ACCESS MODEL / AVAILABILITY / COST / PLATFORM / OTHER]

SEARCH STRATEGY
Search for:
- commercially published alternatives
- academic or university press titles
- reputable trade titles
- OER or public-domain options when appropriate
- titles already documented as library-supported ZTC at another institution, when evidence exists

Do not search only for near-identical titles. Search for resources that accomplish the same instructional job.

FOR EACH CANDIDATE
Assess:
- instructional match
- likely college-level appropriateness
- publisher
- public evidence of academic-library availability
- public evidence of ZTC use elsewhere, if any
- important differences from the requested title
- local verification still needed

OUTPUT
Table:
Candidate | Instructional Match | Evidence | Difference From Requested Title | Local Check

Then:
1. Rank the strongest 5.
2. Identify the top 2 most likely to be worth vendor checking first.
3. Include one OER option if a credible match exists.
4. Identify any important content that none of the alternatives appears to replace.

Do not invent licensing terms to make an alternative look more promising.
Useful framing for faculty:

“The exact title may not have an access model that works for a full class, but I can look for alternatives that serve the same instructional purpose.”

Librarian 4

Compare Library Access Options

Use this after you have gathered actual local vendor information. AI can organize the comparison, but it should work only from the data you provide.

🌱 Quick Prompt

Best when you want a clean table from messy vendor notes.

I am an academic librarian comparing actual access options for a possible ZTC course resource.

Here are my verified vendor/library notes:
[PASTE VERIFIED DATA]

Organize the information into a table:

Option | Price | User Model | Perpetual or Subscription | DRM/Download Limits | Accessibility Notes | Main Strength | Main Risk

Use only the data I provide.

Do not fill in missing information from general knowledge.

If something is missing, write "Not provided."

Then identify:
- the option that appears most sustainable
- the option with the lowest access risk for a whole class
- any important question I still need answered before recommending a purchase
🚀 Deeper Workflow

Best when cost, sustainability, student access, and backup planning all matter.

I am an academic librarian comparing verified local acquisition/access options for a potential ZTC course resource.

VERIFIED DATA
[PASTE PRICE, VENDOR, USER MODEL, TERM, DRM, PLATFORM, ACCESSIBILITY, HOLDINGS, AND OTHER NOTES]

COURSE CONTEXT
[COURSE / APPROXIMATE ENROLLMENT / EXPECTED FREQUENCY OF USE]

Use only the verified data I provide.

Compare each option on:
- whole-class access risk
- sustainability across terms
- total cost
- recurring vs. one-time cost
- simultaneous-user limitations
- DRM/download friction
- accessibility information provided
- platform complexity
- backup risk if subscription access ends

Do not infer missing facts.

OUTPUT
Create:
Option | Verified Terms | Whole-Class Fit | Sustainability | Main Risk | Missing Information

Then provide:
1. Strongest option if reliable whole-class access is the priority
2. Strongest option if long-term sustainability is the priority
3. Strongest option if lowest cost is the priority
4. Any option I should rule out for this use case
5. Questions that remain unresolved

Do not create a recommendation based on features I did not provide.
Librarian 5

Turn the Research Into a Clear Faculty Reply

Use this when the research is done but the faculty member does not need a paragraph about DRM, concurrent-user models, or acquisitions systems.

🌱 Quick Prompt

Best when you want a short, collegial email.

I am an academic librarian replying to a faculty member about a possible ZTC resource.

Here are my verified findings:
[PASTE FINDINGS]

Draft a brief, collegial email.

The email should:
- answer the faculty member's main question first
- explain any access limitation in plain language
- avoid unnecessary acquisitions jargon
- identify the best available option
- identify any alternative worth considering
- clearly say what is confirmed and what is still pending
- give the faculty member one clear next step, if needed

Do not add facts that are not in my findings.

Keep the tone supportive and practical.
🚀 Deeper Workflow

Best when there are several options and you want the explanation to support an actual teaching decision.

I am an academic librarian communicating verified ZTC/library findings to a faculty member.

FACULTY REQUEST
[PASTE OR SUMMARIZE]

VERIFIED FINDINGS
[PASTE]

ALTERNATIVES
[OPTIONAL]

Draft a concise faculty-facing response that:

1. Opens with the practical answer.
2. Explains the status of the requested title.
3. Translates technical access terms into plain language.
4. Explains why any limitation matters for students in a full class.
5. Presents the strongest alternative(s), if available.
6. Distinguishes confirmed access from possibilities that still require work.
7. Recommends a next step without pressuring the faculty member to change materials.

Avoid:
- unexplained vendor jargon
- implying that OER is always the preferred answer
- presenting a library alternative as guaranteed if access is not confirmed
- overwhelming the faculty member with every detail from the research

At the end, include a compact internal note for me listing any follow-up I still need to do. Keep that internal note separate from the faculty email.
