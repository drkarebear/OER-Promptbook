Avoid rebuilding from scratch

Fit OER Into the Course You Already Teach

Once you have promising resources, AI can help with the next time-consuming step: matching them to your weeks, units, outcomes, and existing course structure while keeping student workload realistic.

  • ✓ Map materials week by week
  • ✓ Protect student workload
  • ✓ Preserve what already works

Start With Your Existing Course, Not a Blank Page

Paste your weekly topics, syllabus, modules, COR, or current textbook schedule. Then let AI suggest where OER could fit before you start moving anything around.

Good default: ask for no more than about one hour of assigned reading per week, unless your course intentionally requires more. You can change that limit in any prompt below.

What Are You Trying to Plan?

📅 Map Readings Week by Week Match readings to weekly topics with a workload limit. ↔ Replace a Textbook Chapter by Chapter Keep your current sequence while swapping in OER. 🗂️ Build a Reading Playlist Combine several resources into one coherent course path. ⏱️ Check Workload and Gaps Spot overloaded weeks, repetition, and missing coverage.
Plan 1

Map OER Readings to Your Course Week by Week

Start with the course you already have. If you already found possible OER, AI can match the strongest readings to each week. If you have not found readings yet, AI can turn your schedule into a focused reading-needs map for your next search.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when you want a brief weekly chart without redesigning the course.

Keep this simple and focused.

I teach [COURSE NAME] at a community college.

Here are my weekly topics or units:
[PASTE YOUR WEEKLY SCHEDULE OR UNIT LIST]

If I already have OER readings or resources I am considering, they are here:
[PASTE TITLES, LINKS, TABLE OF CONTENTS, OR UPLOAD MATERIALS — OR LEAVE BLANK]

Help me make a week-by-week reading plan.

If I provided possible readings, match the strongest reading or readings to each week.

If I did NOT provide readings, do not invent specific resources. Instead, tell me what kind of reading I should look for each week so I can use that as an OER search plan.

Keep required reading to a maximum of 60 minutes per week. This is a ceiling, not a target. If a week's goal can be met with less reading, recommend less.

Create a brief table.

If I provided readings:
Week | Topic | Suggested Reading | Why It Fits | Approx. Reading-Time Range

If I did not provide readings:
Week | Topic | What Students Need | Ideal Reading Type | Target Reading-Time Range

Important:
- Do not force a reading into a week if it does not genuinely fit.
- Do not invent chapters, page numbers, links, or reading lengths.
- If you cannot estimate reading time reliably, write “Cannot estimate reliably.”
- Avoid assigning several readings that do essentially the same job.
- If I provided resources and a week has no strong match, write “Gap — needs another resource.”
- Keep the plan simple enough that I can review it quickly.

At the end, tell me which 2–3 weeks need the most attention from me.
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Build a Deeper Map

Best when you want alignment, workload, and gaps considered together.

Build a week-by-week OER reading plan for my existing community college course.

COURSE STRUCTURE
[PASTE WEEKLY TOPICS, UNITS, MODULES, OR SYLLABUS SCHEDULE]

COURSE OUTCOMES OR MAJOR GOALS
[OPTIONAL BUT RECOMMENDED]

OER MATERIALS AVAILABLE
[UPLOAD OR PASTE TITLES, LINKS, TABLES OF CONTENTS, CHAPTER LISTS, OR OTHER RESOURCE INFORMATION — OR LEAVE BLANK IF I AM STILL LOOKING]

WORKLOAD CONSTRAINT
Keep required reading to a maximum of [60] minutes per week. Treat this as a ceiling, not a target. If the instructional goal can be met with less reading, recommend less.

PROCESS
1. Identify the main instructional purpose of each week.
2. If I provided OER materials, match only readings that directly support that purpose.
3. If I did not provide OER materials, do not invent titles or links. Instead, identify the specific kind of reading I should search for.
4. Use a reasonable reading-time range only when the text, word count, length, and difficulty provide enough evidence. Otherwise write “Cannot estimate reliably.”
5. Avoid unnecessary duplication.
6. Flag weeks with no strong resource or an unclear reading need.
7. Flag weeks where the available OER would create too much reading.
8. Distinguish required from optional/enrichment material when that would improve workload.

OUTPUT IF I PROVIDED OER
Create a table:
Week | Topic/Goal | Required OER | Optional OER | Approx. Required Time | Why It Fits | Gap/Concern

OUTPUT IF I DID NOT PROVIDE OER
Create a table:
Week | Topic/Goal | What Students Need to Learn/Do | Ideal Reading Type | Target Time Range | Search Terms or Resource Need

Then provide:
- 3 weeks with the strongest alignment or clearest reading needs
- 3 weeks needing the most faculty attention
- places where less reading would be enough
- content that appears repeatedly and could be trimmed
- important course needs that remain uncovered
- a short list of targeted OER searches I should run next

Do not invent chapter contents, page numbers, links, titles, or reading times. If evidence is insufficient, say so.
Want a different workload limit?

Replace “60 minutes” with the maximum that fits your course, such as 30, 45, 75, or 90 minutes. Treat the number as a ceiling, not a quota. You can also ask AI to separate required and optional reading.

Plan 2

Replace a Textbook Chapter by Chapter

Use this when your current textbook already gives the course a structure you like. Instead of rebuilding the sequence, ask AI to map open alternatives onto it.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when you can paste your current table of contents.

Keep this simple and focused.

I currently use this textbook or sequence:
[PASTE THE TABLE OF CONTENTS OR WEEKLY CHAPTER LIST]

I have these OER resources available:
[PASTE OR UPLOAD THE OER MATERIALS]

Help me see what could replace each chapter or topic.

Create a table:

Current Chapter/Topic | Possible OER Replacement | Strong Match / Partial Match / No Match | Notes

Do not claim an OER is equivalent unless the content supports that.

If the match is partial, briefly explain what is missing.

If there is no good match, write "Needs another resource."

Do not invent OER contents or textbook contents.

At the end, tell me:
- which replacements look easiest
- which ones would require supplementation
- which parts of my current course would be hardest to replace
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Build a Replacement Map

Best when you want to preserve course architecture but diagnose the labor each replacement would require.

Help me map OER onto the structure of a course currently organized around a commercial or non-open textbook.

CURRENT COURSE/TEXTBOOK STRUCTURE
[PASTE TABLE OF CONTENTS, CHAPTER SEQUENCE, OR WEEKLY READING PLAN]

AVAILABLE OER
[UPLOAD OR PASTE OER TITLES, LINKS, CHAPTER LISTS, OR FULL MATERIALS]

TASK
For each current chapter or major topic:
1. Identify the closest OER coverage.
2. Rate the match as Strong / Partial / Weak / None.
3. Identify important content lost in the replacement.
4. Identify unnecessary content the OER would add.
5. Estimate whether the replacement requires:
   - little or no adaptation
   - light supplementation
   - moderate redesign
   - substantial redesign

OUTPUT
Create a table:
Current Topic | OER Replacement | Match | Missing Content | Extra Content | Faculty Work | Notes

Then identify:
- easiest substitutions
- substitutions most likely to increase prep time
- places where a small supplemental OER could solve the gap
- places where keeping the current structure may no longer make pedagogical sense

Be candid. The goal is not to force a one-to-one replacement if a different OER structure would work better.
Plan 3

Build a Coherent Reading Playlist

Use this when no single textbook is perfect and you want a small set of readings that feels intentional rather than like a folder full of links.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when you have several resources and want help choosing what actually earns a place.

Keep this simple and focused.

I teach:
[COURSE]

Here are the major units or topics:
[PASTE UNIT LIST]

Here are OER readings I am considering:
[PASTE TITLES, LINKS, OR UPLOAD MATERIALS]

Help me build a simple reading playlist for the course.

For each unit, choose only the readings that add something important.

Create a table:
Unit | Required Reading | Optional Reading | Why It Belongs

Keep the total reading workload reasonable and avoid assigning several resources that say essentially the same thing.

If a resource does not earn a clear place in the course, leave it out.

If a unit still has a gap, label it clearly.

At the end, list any resources you recommend removing because they are redundant, too long, or not closely aligned.
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Curate the Course

Best when you want coherence, sequencing, and student workload treated as design constraints.

Curate a coherent OER reading playlist for this community college course.

COURSE GOALS / UNITS
[PASTE]

CANDIDATE OER
[UPLOAD OR PASTE]

DESIGN PRINCIPLES
- Prefer fewer, stronger resources over a large collection.
- Each required reading should have a clear instructional purpose.
- Avoid redundancy unless repetition is intentional.
- Sequence readings so concepts build logically.
- Keep workload within [60] minutes of required reading per week unless I specify otherwise.
- Use optional/enrichment materials when something is valuable but not essential.

FOR EACH RESOURCE
Assess:
- what unique contribution it makes
- where it fits best
- whether it duplicates another resource
- whether it is too long for the value it adds
- whether it appears necessary, optional, or removable

OUTPUT
Create a course playlist table:
Week/Unit | Instructional Goal | Required OER | Optional OER | Est. Time | Why This Sequence Works

Then provide:
- resources to keep
- resources to cut
- resources to shorten or excerpt, if licensing permits
- remaining gaps
- places where the sequence may need instructor-created transitions or context

Do not maximize the number of resources. Optimize for coherence, usefulness, and manageable student workload.
Plan 4

Check Workload, Repetition, and Course Gaps

Use this after you have a draft plan. AI can help identify the weeks where students may be overloaded and the places where your new zero-cost course still needs attention.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when you want a quick sanity check before finalizing the schedule.

Keep this simple and focused.

Here is my draft course reading plan:
[PASTE WEEK-BY-WEEK PLAN]

Please check it for:
- weeks with too much reading
- weeks with very little reading
- repeated content
- topics that seem missing
- readings that do not clearly fit the week's goal

My target is about [60] minutes of assigned reading per week.

Create a short table:
Week | Workload | Possible Issue | Suggested Adjustment

Do not invent reading lengths if they are not available.

If you are unsure, label the estimate as uncertain.

At the end, tell me the 3 changes most likely to make the course more manageable for students.
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Audit the Plan

Best when you want to balance workload, alignment, sequence, and faculty labor before launch.

Audit this draft OER/ZTC course plan for student workload, coherence, and remaining design problems.

COURSE PLAN
[PASTE WEEK-BY-WEEK SCHEDULE, READINGS, ACTIVITIES, AND OPTIONAL MATERIALS]

COURSE GOALS
[OPTIONAL]

TARGET READING WORKLOAD
[60] minutes of required reading per week.

AUDIT FOR
1. Overloaded weeks
2. Underdeveloped weeks
3. Redundant content
4. Gaps in course coverage
5. Weak sequencing
6. Readings that do not earn their place
7. Weeks where students are asked to learn too many new concepts at once
8. Places where optional material should be separated from required material
9. Places likely to create unnecessary faculty prep or explanation

OUTPUT
Create a table:
Week | Goal | Est. Workload | Strength | Concern | Suggested Adjustment

Then identify:
- highest-priority workload fixes
- content that can be cut without meaningful loss
- gaps that still need another resource
- weeks where instructor context or transition may be more useful than adding another reading
- any places where the plan appears to be recreating the complexity of a commercial textbook rather than simplifying the student experience

Use cautious estimates and state uncertainty clearly.

Before You Publish the New Plan

A neat AI-generated table can make a course look finished before it actually is. Do one final human pass.

  • Check the readings themselves. Make sure the assigned sections really contain what the plan says they do.
  • Check workload in context. Reading time is only one part of student labor; include writing, discussion, practice, and major assignments in your judgment.
  • Keep transitions in mind. A course built from multiple OER may need short instructor introductions that explain how the pieces connect.
  • Trim aggressively. Zero-cost materials still cost students time. Every required resource should earn its place.

What Next?

Still have an empty week? Find one missing resource. Unsure about a resource in the plan? Evaluate it. Can't fill a gap with OER? Explore other ZTC options.
