Responsible use

Using AI Thoughtfully

The OER Promptbook encourages experimentation with AI when it can meaningfully reduce faculty workload. It also recognizes that generative AI has real environmental, labor, bias, privacy, and intellectual-property concerns.

Why Use AI If AI Is Problematic?

That tension is worth naming. AI is already shaping education and work, and learning how to use it critically can be valuable. At the same time, useful technology is not automatically ethical, neutral, or cost-free.

The goal of The OER Promptbook is not maximum AI use. It is purposeful AI use. We use AI where a focused prompt may reduce repetitive searching, comparison, or organization work, while keeping academic and professional decisions with people.

Principles for Using the Promptbook

These principles are informed by the PAIRR project's approach to critical, ethical, and informed AI use.

1

Use AI for a Reason

Use AI when it meaningfully reduces work or opens a useful line of inquiry. If an ordinary search, library database, spreadsheet, or other simpler tool can do the job just as well, use that instead.

2

Keep Human Expertise in Charge

AI can organize possibilities. Faculty decide what belongs in a course. Librarians verify access and licensing. Accessibility professionals, instructional designers, and other experts should guide decisions in their areas of expertise.

3

Expect Bias, Not Neutrality

AI models learn patterns from human-produced material that reflects unequal representation, stereotypes, linguistic bias, and other social inequities. Treat AI output as something to examine critically, not as an objective answer.

4

Protect Privacy, IP, and Human Work

Do not paste confidential student, employee, or institutional information into public AI systems. Think carefully before uploading writing, images, or other work that you do not own or have permission to share.

5

Verify What Matters

AI can sound confident while being wrong. Open the sources, verify licenses and links, check accessibility claims, confirm library access locally, and distinguish what is documented from what is merely plausible.

6

Use Enough AI, Not More AI

Repeated generations, oversized outputs, and resource-intensive image or video generation are not automatically better. Ask for the smallest useful result, then stop when the task is solved.

A useful Promptbook question: “Will AI meaningfully reduce the labor of this task?”

If the answer is no, you probably do not need AI for it.

The Costs Are Bigger Than One Prompt

PAIRR's ethics statement emphasizes several connected concerns that are easy to lose sight of when AI appears as a simple text box.

Environmental Impact

Generative AI depends on data centers that require energy, water, land, hardware, and mined resources. Different AI tasks can have very different resource demands, and precise estimates are difficult because companies do not disclose enough information.

That uncertainty is a reason for thoughtful use, not a reason to invent a simple per-prompt environmental number.

Labor and Human Expertise

AI systems rely on human writing, expertise, software development, data labeling, moderation, and other forms of labor that can become invisible when we describe everything simply as “AI.”

Professional communities should have a voice in deciding where AI assists human work and where it should not replace it.

Bias and Representation

The material used to train AI is not a neutral record of human knowledge. Some communities, languages, dialects, and perspectives are overrepresented while others are missing, marginalized, or stereotyped.

This matters when AI recommends texts, authors, examples, perspectives, or standards of “good” academic language.

Privacy, Intellectual Property, and Voice

Human writing and creative work represent time, knowledge, identity, and expertise. Treating that work only as training “data” can obscure questions of permission, ownership, compensation, and control.

For the Promptbook, this means protecting confidential information and being cautious about uploading other people's work into public systems.

What Thoughtful Use Can Look Like

  • Ask for a shortlist, not 50 results. More output is not necessarily more useful.
  • Use text when text will do. Do not generate images or video for a task that only needs a few sentences or a table.
  • Choose efficient tools or models when appropriate. A complex reasoning workflow is not necessary for every search.
  • Improve one prompt instead of repeatedly starting over. Deliberate iteration can reduce unnecessary generations and improve results.
  • Stop when you have enough evidence to make the next human decision. AI does not need to make the final call.
  • Advocate beyond individual choices. Responsible AI also requires institutional policy, transparency, sustainable infrastructure, fair labor practices, and meaningful public oversight.

This page is informed by and adapts ideas from AI is Problematic. Why Does PAIRR Use it?, written by Julie Gamberg, Marit MacArthur, Anna Mills, Aparna Sinha, and Lisa Sperber for the Peer and AI Review + Reflection (PAIRR) project.

Keep Experimenting. Keep Questioning.

Responsible AI use is not a checklist we finish once. Technologies, evidence, policies, and institutional practices will continue to change. The Promptbook encourages faculty to experiment while remaining skeptical, informed, and willing to decide that AI is not the right tool for a particular task.
