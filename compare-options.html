See the tradeoffs at a glance

Stop Opening Ten Tabs Just to Compare Your Options

Once you have several promising resources, use AI to organize the evidence side by side so you can focus your own review on the differences that actually affect adoption.

  • ✓ Narrow the shortlist
  • ✓ See meaningful tradeoffs
  • ✓ Avoid fake precision

Comparison Should Make the Decision Easier, Not Create More Homework

These prompts ask AI to organize evidence and surface tradeoffs. They deliberately avoid turning every decision into a giant 100-point scoring system.

Tip: Tell AI which factors matter most to you. A resource with the broadest coverage is not always the best choice if it creates more student or faculty workload.

What Are You Comparing?

↔ Two or More OER Compare course fit, quality, usability, and gaps. ⚖️ OER vs. Library/ZTC Compare openness, access, sustainability, and instructional fit. 🗂️ Course Plans or Core + Supplement Compare whole-course combinations or test whether one targeted supplement fixes a documented gap. ✓ Make a Shortlist or Decision Memo Turn comparison findings into a practical next step.
Compare 1

Compare Two or More OER

Use this when several resources look promising and you want to know where they meaningfully differ before reading all of them in depth.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when you want a short table and a clear recommendation about what to review first.

Keep this simple and focused.

I teach:
[COURSE NAME AND SHORT DESCRIPTION]

I am considering these OER:
[PASTE LINKS, TITLES, OR UPLOAD THE RESOURCES]

Compare them in a simple table using:
- Course coverage
- Level and readability
- Organization
- Activities or learning supports
- Currency, if relevant
- Accessibility information that can actually be verified
- Open license
- Biggest strength
- Biggest gap

Do not invent features, licenses, accessibility claims, or content.

If you cannot verify something, write "Needs verification."

After the table:
1. Tell me which resource seems strongest overall for my course.
2. Tell me which one I should review first.
3. Explain the most important tradeoff between the top two.

Keep the answer concise.
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Compare Against Course Needs

Best when you want a deeper comparison grounded in your COR, outcomes, or course plan.

Conduct a decision-focused comparison of these OER for my community college course.

COURSE CONTEXT
[COURSE DESCRIPTION, OUTCOMES, TOPICS, OR SYLLABUS]

CANDIDATE OER
[UPLOAD OR LINK TO EACH RESOURCE]

PHASE 1: DEFINE THE DECISION
Extract the 6–10 course needs that matter most for adoption.

PHASE 2: COMPARE
Compare each OER on:
- coverage of major course needs
- depth and level
- organization and assignability
- clarity and usability
- currency where it matters
- activities, exercises, or ancillaries
- documented accessibility information
- license and remix flexibility
- likely faculty adaptation work
- likely student workload

EVIDENCE RULES
- Point to specific chapters, sections, or source evidence when possible.
- Do not invent features or claims.
- Mark uncertainty clearly.
- Do not create a fake numerical score if the evidence does not support one.

OUTPUT
Create a comparison table:
Criterion | OER A | OER B | OER C (if applicable) | Why This Difference Matters

Then provide:
1. Strongest overall candidate
2. Best candidate if minimizing faculty redesign is the priority
3. Best candidate if flexibility/remixing is the priority
4. Most consequential gap in each resource
5. The 3 sections I should personally inspect before deciding

Be candid if there is no clear winner.
Have one deal-breaker?

Add: “For me, [ACCESSIBILITY / LOW READING LOAD / REMIXABILITY / ANCILLARIES / COURSE ALIGNMENT] matters more than the other criteria. Make that priority explicit in the comparison.”

Compare 2

Compare an OER With a Library-Supported ZTC Option

Use this when the choice is not simply “free vs. paid.” Compare openness, long-term access, course fit, licensing, and workload.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when you have one open option and one possible library option.

Keep this simple and focused.

I teach:
[COURSE]

I am considering:

OPTION A — OER
[LINK OR INFORMATION]

OPTION B — LIBRARY/ZTC RESOURCE
[LINK OR INFORMATION]

Compare them using:
- Fit with my course
- Student cost
- Ease of student access
- Whether access depends on my library
- Open license or reuse flexibility
- Sustainability across future semesters
- Faculty prep or adaptation work
- Biggest strength
- Biggest risk

Important:
Do not assume the library resource is available to my students unless my institution has verified it.

Do not assume "free online" means OER.

At the end, explain:
- when Option A would be the better choice
- when Option B would be the better choice
- what I need to verify before deciding
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Compare Sustainability and Risk

Best when access model and long-term course stability matter as much as content.

Compare an OER option with a library-supported or other ZTC option for this community college course.

COURSE
[COURSE CONTEXT]

OPTION A — OER
[RESOURCE]

OPTION B — LIBRARY/ZTC
[RESOURCE]

COMPARE THESE DIMENSIONS

INSTRUCTIONAL FIT
- coverage
- depth
- organization
- student usability
- ancillaries or learning supports

ACCESS AND SUSTAINABILITY
- student cost
- institution-dependent access
- simultaneous-user or user-limit risk
- subscription vs. perpetual access if known
- platform friction
- likelihood that access could change

OPENNESS AND FACULTY CONTROL
- reuse
- revision
- remixing
- redistribution
- ability to customize or excerpt

IMPLEMENTATION LABOR
- faculty redesign
- LMS setup
- student instructions
- backup planning

VERIFICATION RULES
Do not infer institutional library access, license terms, pricing, or user limits without evidence.

OUTPUT
Create a table:
Dimension | OER Option | Library/ZTC Option | Decision Implication

Then identify:
- strongest option for instructional fit
- strongest option for sustainability
- strongest option for faculty control
- biggest risk in each option
- what must be verified locally
- your provisional recommendation, with conditions

Do not treat OER as automatically superior or library content as automatically more polished.
Compare 3

Compare Whole-Course Plans or Test a Core + Supplement

Use this when the real decision is between combinations of materials, or when one promising core OER may need only a carefully targeted supplement.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when you have two possible course plans and want a quick sanity check.

Keep this simple and focused.

I am choosing between two course-material plans.

PLAN A
[PASTE MATERIALS, WEEKLY MAP, OR SHORT DESCRIPTION]

PLAN B
[PASTE MATERIALS, WEEKLY MAP, OR SHORT DESCRIPTION]

Compare them for:
- Coverage of the course
- Number of separate resources/platforms students must use
- Student reading workload
- Ease of navigation
- Gaps
- Faculty setup or adaptation work
- Sustainability
- Anything that still needs licensing or access verification

Create a short table.

Then tell me:
1. Which plan seems simpler for students
2. Which plan seems simpler for me to maintain
3. Which plan seems stronger academically
4. The biggest tradeoff I should think about

Do not invent information that is not in the plans.
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Compare Course Architecture

Best when the options differ in sequence, workload, platform complexity, and faculty labor.

Compare these two proposed OER/ZTC course-material architectures.

COURSE GOALS
[PASTE]

PLAN A
[PASTE WEEKLY MATERIALS, PLATFORMS, AND OPTIONAL/REQUIRED ITEMS]

PLAN B
[PASTE WEEKLY MATERIALS, PLATFORMS, AND OPTIONAL/REQUIRED ITEMS]

COMPARE
1. Course coverage and alignment
2. Sequence and coherence
3. Student cognitive/load burden
4. Reading workload
5. Number of platforms and navigation steps
6. Required vs. optional clarity
7. Faculty setup and maintenance labor
8. Dependence on institution-specific licenses
9. Sustainability across future terms
10. Backup options if a resource disappears

OUTPUT
Create a table:
Dimension | Plan A | Plan B | Meaningful Difference

Then identify:
- better student experience
- lower faculty maintenance burden
- stronger academic coherence
- greater access risk
- unnecessary complexity in either plan
- elements from each plan worth combining into a stronger hybrid

If a hybrid is clearly better, propose the simplest hybrid rather than forcing a winner.

Test a Core OER + One Targeted Supplement

Use this when the core resource is strong but has one documented gap. The goal is to see whether one supplement earns its place without turning the course into a patchwork.

I have one promising core OER, but it has a documented gap. Help me test whether ONE targeted supplement is a better solution than replacing the core resource or building a complicated package.

COURSE CONTEXT
[PASTE COURSE, UNIT, OR OUTCOME INFORMATION]

CORE OER
[PASTE LINK, TITLE, OR RELEVANT INFORMATION]

DOCUMENTED GAP
[DESCRIBE WHAT THE CORE RESOURCE DOES NOT DO WELL ENOUGH]

POSSIBLE SUPPLEMENT
[PASTE THE RESOURCE, LINK, OR DESCRIPTION IF I HAVE ONE]

Check:
- Does the supplement directly solve the documented gap?
- Does it duplicate material students already have?
- How much additional reading or activity time does it add?
- Does it add another platform, login, or navigation burden?
- Are its access, accessibility, and licensing conditions clear?
- Will it create significant faculty setup or maintenance work?
- Is the improvement large enough to justify the added complexity?

Create a compact table:
Question | Core Alone | Core + Supplement | Why It Matters

Then give me one of these conclusions:
- Core alone is sufficient
- Core + supplement is worth it
- Keep searching for a better solution

Do not invent features, licenses, accessibility claims, or content. If evidence is missing, say what I still need to verify.
Compare 4

Turn the Comparison Into a Shortlist or Decision Memo

Use this when you already have notes, tables, or evaluation results and want a concise summary that supports an actual faculty decision.

🌱 Quick Prompt — Keep It Simple

Best when your notes are messy and you want a short, usable recommendation.

Keep this simple and focused.

Below are my notes about several OER or ZTC options:
[PASTE NOTES OR COMPARISON RESULTS]

Organize them into a short decision summary.

Give me:

TOP 3 OPTIONS
For each:
- Why it is promising
- Biggest concern
- What I still need to verify

THEN
- Which option I should review first
- Which option is the best backup
- Which option I can probably stop spending time on

Do not add facts that are not in my notes.

If the evidence is too incomplete to recommend one option, tell me what information is missing.
🚀 Deeper Workflow — Write a Decision Brief

Best when you need to preserve uncertainty and explain why one option deserves the next round of faculty review.

Turn the evidence below into a concise faculty decision brief.

EVIDENCE
[PASTE COMPARISON TABLES, NOTES, EVALUATIONS, LINKS, OR VERIFICATION RESULTS]

MY PRIORITIES
[OPTIONAL: COURSE FIT / ACCESSIBILITY / LOW STUDENT WORKLOAD / LOW FACULTY LABOR / REMIXABILITY / SUSTAINABILITY / OTHER]

RULES
- Use only the evidence I provide.
- Preserve uncertainty.
- Do not convert missing evidence into a negative judgment.
- Do not create a numerical score unless I explicitly ask for one.
- Distinguish "not verified" from "does not have."

OUTPUT

1. DECISION AT A GLANCE
A 3–5 sentence summary.

2. SHORTLIST
Option | Best Reason to Consider | Main Tradeoff | Verification Still Needed

3. RECOMMENDED NEXT STEP
Identify:
- first option to review
- backup option
- option to set aside, if justified

4. WHAT WOULD CHANGE THE DECISION?
List the 2–4 missing facts that could reasonably change the recommendation.

5. FACULTY CHECK
Identify what requires my own academic judgment rather than additional AI analysis.

Keep the brief concise enough that I could share it with a colleague or OER/ZTC team.

Think You've Chosen? Stress-Test the Decision

Use this after you have a likely winner. The goal is not to reopen the entire search; it is to catch an overlooked risk before you commit.

Act as a skeptical second reviewer of my tentative OER/ZTC choice.

MY LIKELY CHOICE
[PASTE THE RESOURCE OR COURSE-MATERIAL PLAN]

WHY I AM LEANING THIS WAY
[PASTE MY REASONS, COMPARISON TABLE, OR NOTES]

COURSE CONTEXT
[OPTIONAL: COURSE GOALS, STUDENT NEEDS, OR CONSTRAINTS]

Do not restart the search or argue against the choice just to be contrarian. Instead, look for important things I may be underestimating or overlooking:
- a major content or alignment gap
- unnecessary supplementation
- student reading or navigation burden
- accessibility or access uncertainty
- licensing or long-term availability concerns
- hidden faculty adaptation or maintenance labor
- weak or missing evidence behind my reasoning
- an alternative I dismissed for a reason that is not actually supported

Give me:
1. The 3 most important risks or unanswered questions, if any
2. What evidence would resolve each one
3. Anything that is probably NOT worth worrying about
4. A final judgment: Proceed / Proceed after verifying specific items / Reconsider

If the choice is well supported, say so. Do not manufacture objections.

Before You Choose

A comparison is useful only if the evidence underneath it is sound. Treat the table and stress-test as ways to focus your review, not as automatic decision makers.

  • Open the strongest candidates yourself. The top recommendation should earn a real faculty review.
  • Watch for missing evidence. “Not verified” is not the same as “does not have.”
  • Do not let the longest feature list win. Course fit, student experience, and sustainability may matter more.
  • Keep workload visible. The best academic resource may still be a poor adoption if it creates excessive student or faculty labor.

What Next?

Need more evidence? Evaluate the strongest candidate. Made a choice? Map it into your course. Want a deeper custom workflow? Use Advanced Workflows.
